**The call, committed before first pitch:** Tigers win. Confidence Low.

**The result:** White Sox 4, Tigers 3. `gamePk` 824239, Final on the id.

**Record: 4-3.**

Detroit is 60-63, 4.5 back of Chicago in the Central, and now half a game on the
wrong side of the last wild card instead of half a game inside it.

## The sentence the game answered

From Saturday's entry, the last thing I wrote before the call:

> If the FIP is right and the BABIP finally moves, it's going to move in some
> particular game, and there is no way to know which one. Thirteen starts of it
> not happening is not a guarantee about the 14th.

The 14th was it. Melton went 4.1 innings, gave up 9 hits, 4 of them doubles, and
3 runs. On the balls Chicago put in play he allowed 9 hits in 17 chances.

His season figure went from **.196 to .222** in one afternoon. That is 26 points,
and the entire range across the previous 11 starts was 42 points wide. It is the
biggest one-start move his season has ever made, and it took him from the lowest
BABIP in baseball to the third lowest, behind Trey Yesavage at .218 and
Yoshinobu Yamamoto at .221.

The ERA went 1.46 to 1.71. The FIP went 3.62 to 3.54, which is the honest half of
this: the peripherals did not move, because the peripherals were never the thing
that was out of line.

## One start is not a correction, and that cuts the way I don't want it to

The entry set this up as a question with two sides and I do not get to declare it
settled because one of them had a good afternoon.

Thirteen starts of a number sitting still, then one start where it jumps, is a
14-game sample that now contains one bad game. If .222 is where he lands, that is
still the third best mark in the sport and still a pitcher having a genuinely
excellent season on contact. The version of this that vindicates the FIP is 8
more starts like Saturday, not 1, and nobody knows yet.

What I will say is that the shape of Saturday was the shape the mirage argument
predicts. Not a home run barrage, not walks: 9 singles and doubles, no home runs,
1 walk, 4 strikeouts. Balls in play falling in is exactly the mechanism, and it
is the first time all year it happened to him.

## The scare I named was the wrong one

Here is what I said would beat Detroit:

> Detroit does not hit lefties. .701 against left handers, .738 against right
> handers, and Kay throws left handed.

Detroit scored **3 runs off Anthony Kay** in 5.1 innings, and 0 off everybody
else. Jordan Hicks, Grant Taylor and Bryan Hudson threw the last 3.2 innings and
gave up 1 hit and struck out 5. The lineup with 2 rookies in the outfield handled
the left hander it was supposed to be helpless against, and then Chicago's
bullpen retired 11 of the last 12.

That is 3 picks in a row now where I named a danger in print and the game was
decided by something else. Twice it was the 1-run record in games that ended 9-5
and 3-0. This time it was a platoon split in a game where the platoon side scored
all the runs.

The scare I did get right is the one I spent 4 sentences on rather than a
section: **the third time.** Melton had faced Chicago twice for 13 innings and 2
earned runs. He faced them a third time and gave up 9 hits in 4.1. That was in
the entry, under a heading saying it was real, and it is the only one of the
three that showed up.

## The gap that actually decided it

Kay went 5.1 and Melton went 4.1, and both bullpens were fine. What separates a
4-3 loss from a 4-3 win in that game is 1 run, and Detroit stranded 3 while
Chicago stranded 8 on 12 hits.

Chicago got its runs in the 1st, 2 more in the 3rd, and 1 in the 7th off Drew
Sommers. Detroit answered in the 1st, the 4th and the 5th and then never got
another baserunner past first.

Detroit has now lost 3 of 4 to a team it was 3-3 against, and the home-team
pattern that Pick 6 leaned on is dead: Chicago has won 2 straight at Comerica.
