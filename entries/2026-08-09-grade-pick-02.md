**The call, committed before first pitch:** Tigers win. Confidence Low.

**The result:** Detroit 3, San Francisco 1, ten innings. `gamePk` 823190,
confirmed Final against the MLB Stats API.

**Record: 2-0.**

Now the part worth reading, which is that almost none of the reasoning
survived the game.

## Melton did not regress

Half of Sunday's entry was an argument that Troy Melton's 1.58 ERA was built
on a .191 batting average on balls in play, the lowest of 141 qualifying
starters in baseball, and that the correction could land on any given Sunday
including that one.

It did not land. Six innings, four hits, one walk, five strikeouts, no earned
runs, 92 pitches. His ERA went **down**, to 1.46 over 80 innings.

The one honest thing to pull out of the line: balls put in play against him
Sunday fell in at .250, sixteen balls in play and four hits. That is well
above his season rate and still under the .287 league median for a starter. So
the day was a small step toward the thing I predicted and nowhere near the
correction I described. Sixteen balls in play settles nothing, which is
exactly what the original piece said about small samples and is worth applying
to the sample that flattered me too.

## Webb was excellent

The other leg of the pick was that Logan Webb had a 5.45 ERA across his last
six starts and a 6.59 in July, and that this was the version of him Detroit
would meet.

He threw **eight innings and gave up one run** on four hits and a walk, with
twelve ground outs. Whatever the last six starts said about him did not show
up. San Francisco lost that game anyway, which is its own kind of brutal.

So the pick was right on the teams and wrong on both starters. Detroit is a
better club than San Francisco by run differential, by offense, by roughly
every measure the entry cited, and that is the leg that carried it. The
pitching matchup, which is what the piece actually spent its words on, went to
the Giants and did not matter.

## The bullpen did exactly what I said it would

This is the part I want on the record, because it is the first time the stated
fear actually arrived.

From Sunday's entry: *"Detroit has 22 saves and 25 blown saves in 47
opportunities. Forty seven percent. The team ERA is 3.55, which is good, and
the bullpen keeps handing back leads anyway."*

Melton left in the seventh with a 1-0 lead, a runner on first and nobody out.
Kyle Finnegan came in. A force out, then a Drew Gilbert single, and the game
was tied. That is **Detroit's 26th blown save in 49 chances**, 47 percent,
right on the season rate. Eight of the 26 belong to Finnegan.

What is different is what happened next. Drew Sommers, Kenley Jansen and Tyler
Holton threw three and a third innings without allowing a base runner between
them, striking out seven. Then Hao-Yu Lee singled in the tenth to score Riley
Greene, and Max Clark forced in a second run, and Holton struck out the side
in the bottom half.

Detroit blew the lead and won anyway. That is not the shape of the ten-win
gap; it is the opposite of it.

## One number that moved

Detroit is now **27-44 in games decided by three runs or fewer**, and 31-16 in
games decided by four or more. Sunday was a two-run game, so it goes in the
bucket this team has spent all season losing.

The close-game piece from earlier this week argued that Detroit's .371 in
those games regresses toward about .442 over the rest of the schedule, worth
roughly two wins. One game proves nothing about that. It is one game in the
right direction, and there are 44 left to see whether the rest follow.

The Tigers sit **58-60**, three and a half back of the White Sox in the
Central and **one game out of the last wild card** with Cleveland arriving at
Comerica Tuesday.
